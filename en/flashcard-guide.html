How to study with flashcards, starting from your notes

A flashcard has a question on one side and an answer on the other. You can make cards on paper or in an app, then try answering before looking at the back.

Start with one small topic from your notes. This guide uses basic statistics examples, but you can adapt the steps to terms, concepts, languages or other material.

1. Choose one part of the material

Pick one subtopic, such as mean, median and mode. Read your notes or learning source first. If something is unclear, mark it to reread or ask about.

For your first attempt, make 5 cards. This is just a starting point to help you begin, not a required number.

2. Put one clear question on each card

Give each card one main task. A question like “Explain statistics” is too broad to check quickly. Split it into more specific questions.

On a small screen, scroll the table sideways to see all columns.

Example flashcard content
Front: questionBack: answer
How do you calculate the mean?Add all the values, then divide by the number of values.
What should you do before finding the median?Sort the data from smallest to largest.
What is the median of 2, 4 and 9?4, because it is the middle value in the sorted data.
What is the mode?The value that appears most often in the data.
What is the mean of 2, 4 and 9?(2 + 4 + 9) ÷ 3 = 5.

Add a short source note to the answer if you may need to check it again, such as “Session 2 notes, page 3”. Match the terms and explanations to your class materials.

3. Try answering before turning the card over

Read the question, then say or write your answer. For a calculation card, work through the steps on paper. Then reveal the answer and compare.

If your answer is incorrect, find where it went wrong. Did you forget a term, skip a step or miss the concept? Return to the original material if needed.

4. Note which cards need another look

Separate cards you cannot answer yet, or note their numbers. Try them again in your next session. Occasionally check cards you could previously answer too.

You do not need a complicated timing pattern. Choose a next review time that fits your schedule, then adjust after trying it.

5. Improve confusing cards

If the same card keeps giving you trouble, check the question too. It may be too broad, or its answer may contain too many things.

Before: “What should I know about the median?”

After:

  • “What is the first step in finding the median?”
  • “How do you find the median when there is an odd number of values?”
  • “How do you find the median when there is an even number of values?”

For an even number of values, the last card’s answer is the average of the two middle values after sorting the data.

An example short session

Try this sequence with your first 5 cards:

  1. Prepare your cards and paper if calculations are involved.
  2. Answer each question before revealing the answer.
  3. Note incorrect or incomplete answers.
  4. Check the relevant section of your class notes.
  5. Improve unclear cards and choose when to review again.

Pause if you are only guessing without understanding the question. Return to the explanation or worked example, then try again once it is clearer.

When do you need other practice?

Flashcards suit questions you can answer and check briefly. For essays, longer problems or presentations, also set aside time to practice that type of task.

For example, after reviewing the mean formula, try a new problem with different data. After reviewing an economics term, write an example of its use in your own words.

Start with one course

Make 5 questions from material you are studying. Check that you can verify the answers, then try one review session.
